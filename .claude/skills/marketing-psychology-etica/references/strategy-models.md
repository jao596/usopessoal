# Strategy models

Mental models for reasoning about a marketing decision, plus the models for how growth compounds or stalls. These are thinking tools, not tactics. A tactic tells you what to do; a model tells you whether the thing you want to do makes sense. Reach for these before you write a brief, set a budget, or pick a channel.

Every entry has four parts. **Mechanism** is why the model is true. **Marketing use** is where it earns its keep in a real decision. **Honest use** is the line that keeps the move fair to the customer. **Backfire** is how the same model fools you or the buyer when it is applied lazily. The backfire section matters most: a model you cannot misuse is a model you do not understand.

## Reasoning models

### First principles
- **Mechanism**: most beliefs are inherited by analogy ("this is how our industry does it"). Stripping a problem down to what you actually know to be true, then rebuilding, removes assumptions that were never checked.
- **Marketing use**: when a channel or format "just doesn't work in our category", rebuild from the buyer's real constraints (what they need to believe, what they can afford, what proof they trust) instead of copying the category's playbook. New angles come from here.
- **Honest use**: rebuild toward what is actually true for the customer, not toward a clever story you wish were true. First principles is a reasoning method, not a licence to invent facts.
- **Backfire**: reasoning from scratch on things that are already well settled wastes time and reinvents worse versions. Use it where the inherited answer is suspect, not everywhere.

### Jobs to be done
- **Mechanism**: people do not buy products, they hire them to make progress in a situation. The "job" is the progress, and it is stable even as products change.
- **Marketing use**: write copy and positioning around the job the buyer is trying to finish, not the features you shipped. It tells you your real competitors (often a workaround or "do nothing"), and it tells you the moment to show up.
- **Honest use**: name the job the customer truly has, including the emotional and social parts, without inflating it into a crisis it is not.
- **Backfire**: invent a grand job to justify a product nobody needs, and you write aspirational copy that tests well internally and converts nobody. The job has to be one real people are already trying to do.

### Circle of competence
- **Mechanism**: judgement is reliable inside the area you genuinely understand and unreliable outside it. The edge of that circle matters more than its size.
- **Marketing use**: play offence where you know the customer, the channel, and the numbers cold. When a decision sits outside your circle (a new market, an unfamiliar channel), buy expertise or run a cheap test before betting real budget.
- **Honest use**: be candid, internally and to clients, about where your knowledge ends. Selling confidence you do not have is where bad campaigns get funded.
- **Backfire**: past wins convince you the circle is bigger than it is, so you apply a tactic that worked in one context to one where the mechanism does not hold. Knowing the boundary is the whole point.

### Inversion
- **Mechanism**: it is often easier to avoid failure than to engineer success. Asking "what would guarantee this flops?" surfaces risks that a "how do we win?" framing hides.
- **Marketing use**: before launch, list everything that would make the campaign fail (unclear offer, wrong audience, a broken step in the funnel) and remove those first. Fixing the guaranteed-losers usually beats chasing a clever winner.
- **Honest use**: invert to protect the customer's experience too. "What would make a buyer feel tricked?" is a fast way to find dark patterns before you ship them.
- **Backfire**: pure avoidance turns into paralysis and beige, risk-free work that offends nobody and moves nobody. Inversion clears the floor; it does not raise the ceiling.

### Occam's razor
- **Mechanism**: among explanations that fit the evidence equally, the one with the fewest assumptions is usually right, because each added assumption is another thing that can be wrong.
- **Marketing use**: when a metric moves, check the simple cause first (tracking broke, a big referrer changed, seasonality) before building an elaborate story. Simpler messages and simpler funnels also usually convert better.
- **Honest use**: prefer the simple honest explanation to the flattering complicated one. "The creative was weak" is often truer than a five-factor attribution theory.
- **Backfire**: "simplest" is not "the one I like". Real buying behaviour is multi-causal, and forcing it into one tidy reason throws away signal. The razor breaks ties between equal fits; it does not let you ignore evidence.

### Pareto / the vital few (80/20)
- **Mechanism**: in most systems, outcomes are unevenly distributed. A small share of inputs drives a large share of results, because effects compound and concentrate rather than spread evenly.
- **Marketing use**: find the few segments, channels, keywords, or creatives that produce most of the return and pour resource there before optimising the long tail. Cut or park the inputs that produce almost nothing.
- **Honest use**: concentrating spend is fine; concentrating manipulation on your most vulnerable few is not. Apply it to allocation, not to squeezing a small group hardest.
- **Backfire**: the split is descriptive, not a law with exact numbers, and it shifts over time. Chase last quarter's vital few forever and you strip out the experiments that were about to become this quarter's winners.

### Local vs global optima
- **Mechanism**: hill-climbing on the nearest metric can leave you stuck on a small peak when a much higher one sits across a valley you refused to cross.
- **Marketing use**: endless A/B tweaks to a landing page climb the local hill. Sometimes the bigger win needs a step down first (a full redesign, a new offer, a different audience) that dips the metric before it climbs past the old ceiling.
- **Honest use**: be honest that a redesign is a bet, and size it so a failed jump does not sink the business. Do not sell a risky leap as a sure thing.
- **Backfire**: assume every plateau is a local optimum and you throw away things that were already near-best, restarting from zero for no gain. Know when you are actually at the top of the tallest hill.

### Theory of constraints
- **Mechanism**: a system's throughput is set by its single tightest bottleneck. Improving anything that is not the constraint does not raise output, it just piles up work in front of the real limit.
- **Marketing use**: find the one stage capping results (traffic, or conversion, or retention, or margin) and fix that. More ad spend is wasted if the checkout is where buyers actually drop.
- **Honest use**: fix the constraint by making the weak stage genuinely better, not by pushing more people through a broken one. Papering over a bad product with more traffic just spreads the disappointment.
- **Backfire**: attack the wrong stage because it is the one you enjoy or know how to fix, and you burn effort while the real bottleneck holds. The constraint also moves once you relieve it, so yesterday's fix is not today's.

### Opportunity cost
- **Mechanism**: every yes is a no to everything else that budget, time, and attention could have done. The true cost of an action is the best alternative you gave up, not the cash you spent.
- **Marketing use**: judge a campaign against the next-best use of the same money, not against zero. A channel with positive return can still be the wrong call if another one returns more per euro.
- **Honest use**: count the customer's opportunity cost too. Their time and money spent with you is time and money not spent elsewhere, which is a reason to make the trade genuinely worth it.
- **Backfire**: obsessing over the road not taken breeds second-guessing and constant strategy-switching, which has its own cost. Price the alternative, decide, and commit.

### Diminishing returns
- **Mechanism**: past some point, each extra unit of input adds less output than the one before, because the easy gains get taken first and saturation sets in.
- **Marketing use**: frequency, spend on a saturated channel, and optimisation effort all curve flat eventually. The signal to diversify or stop is when the next euro clearly returns less than the last.
- **Honest use**: recognise the flat part instead of hammering the same audience harder, which for the customer just reads as being nagged.
- **Backfire**: call it too early and you quit a channel before it compounds; call it too late and you overspend into the flat. The skill is spotting the actual inflection, not assuming one.

### Second-order thinking
- **Mechanism**: actions have consequences, and those consequences have consequences. The first-order effect is obvious; the second and third order are where the real result usually lives.
- **Marketing use**: a discount lifts sales now (first order), but may train buyers to wait for discounts and erode reference price (second order). Play the move forward a few steps before you make it.
- **Honest use**: check whether the downstream effects land on the customer as harm you would not accept if it were visible up front. Short-term wins that create long-term buyer regret are not wins.
- **Backfire**: you can imagine so many downstream effects that you never act, or you invent scary ones to defend the status quo. Go a few steps deep on the effects that are plausible, not infinitely deep on every ghost.

### The map is not the territory
- **Mechanism**: every model, dashboard, and persona is a simplified representation. It is useful precisely because it leaves things out, which means it is always at least a little wrong.
- **Marketing use**: your attribution model, your funnel diagram, and your ideal-customer profile are maps. Treat them as guides to be checked against real buyers, not as the reality itself.
- **Honest use**: when the data and a real customer conversation disagree, do not force the person to fit the dashboard. The territory wins.
- **Backfire**: optimise the metric instead of the thing the metric stands for and you get vanity numbers that look great while the business rots. The map improving is not the territory improving.

### Probabilistic thinking
- **Mechanism**: most marketing outcomes are uncertain, so decisions are bets with odds, not guarantees. Thinking in probabilities and expected value beats thinking in false certainties.
- **Marketing use**: size bets by expected value and downside, run more small tests to update your estimates, and judge a decision by whether the odds were good, not only by whether it happened to work.
- **Honest use**: share the uncertainty honestly with stakeholders and clients. "This has maybe a one-in-three shot at a big return" is more useful and more honest than a confident forecast you cannot support.
- **Backfire**: fake precision (a 73.4% confidence pulled from nowhere) launders a guess as science. And a good process can still lose on any single bet, so do not junk a sound method after one bad outcome.

### Barbell strategy (mostly-proven plus a few asymmetric bets)
- **Mechanism**: put most of your resource in safe, reliable moves and a small slice in high-variance bets with capped downside and large upside. You avoid ruin while staying exposed to the rare huge win. The middle (moderate risk, moderate reward) is where you get the worst of both.
- **Marketing use**: run the bulk of budget on channels and messages you know convert, and reserve a small, fixed percentage for experiments that could break out. Losing the small slice never hurts; landing one pays for all the misses.
- **Honest use**: keep the experiments honest tests, not stunts that mislead buyers to chase virality. The upside you want is a real new channel, not a cheap trick that burns trust.
- **Backfire**: let the "safe" side quietly drift into moderate-risk mediocrity, or let the bet side grow until a loss actually hurts, and you lose the whole point. The shape only works if the two ends stay genuinely separate and the bets stay small.

## Growth and scaling models

### Feedback loops
- **Mechanism**: an output that feeds back as an input creates a loop. Reinforcing loops accelerate a trend (more of X causes more of X); balancing loops resist change and pull toward a set point.
- **Marketing use**: build reinforcing loops on purpose (happy customers refer more customers) and find the balancing loops quietly capping you (rising acquisition cost as you exhaust an audience). Loops, not one-off pushes, are what scale.
- **Honest use**: build loops that get stronger when the customer genuinely benefits, so growth and satisfaction pull the same direction. A loop fed by regret unwinds.
- **Backfire**: reinforcing loops run in reverse just as fast. The same word-of-mouth that compounds growth compounds a bad reputation. And unnoticed balancing loops make you spend harder against a ceiling you cannot see.

### Compounding
- **Mechanism**: when returns build on prior returns, growth is exponential, not linear. Small consistent gains dwarf occasional big pushes because each period starts from a higher base.
- **Marketing use**: assets that accumulate (content, email lists, brand memory, reviews, backlinks, retained customers) compound. Prioritise the work whose value stacks over time over one-off spikes that reset to zero.
- **Honest use**: compounding rewards patience and consistency, which are honest by nature. Let the asset build on real value delivered, not on gamed metrics that decay.
- **Backfire**: the early curve is nearly flat, which tempts you to quit right before it bends. And it compounds bad inputs too: a slowly worsening reputation or a shrinking list erodes exponentially the same way it would have grown.

### Network effects
- **Mechanism**: a product gets more valuable to each user as more users join. Value scales with connections, not just count, so late users get more than early ones did.
- **Marketing use**: if your product has them, growth marketing should target the density that makes the network click (a region, a niche, a use case) rather than thin reach everywhere. Referral and invite mechanics are natural fits.
- **Honest use**: grow the network by making it genuinely more useful as it fills, not by locking people in and calling it value. The effect should be a benefit to members, not a trap.
- **Backfire**: claiming network effects you do not have leads to a "grow at all costs" burn that never reaches the density to pay off. Below critical mass the effect is negative: an empty network is a reason to leave.

### The flywheel
- **Mechanism**: a set of steps arranged so each one feeds the next and the whole loop gets easier to turn with every rotation. Momentum accumulates; stopping and restarting is expensive, so consistent pushes beat sporadic big ones.
- **Marketing use**: map your growth as a loop (attract, convert, satisfy, refer, reinvest) and find the step that, when pushed, spins the whole wheel faster. Fund the loop, not disconnected campaigns.
- **Honest use**: the wheel should turn because customers are genuinely well served at each step. A flywheel built on satisfaction is durable; one built on pressure needs constant re-pushing.
- **Backfire**: a broken step turns the flywheel into an anchor, where effort at one stage leaks out at the next and never compounds. And momentum cuts both ways: a wheel that spins down takes real force to restart.

### Switching costs
- **Mechanism**: the time, money, data, and habit a customer would spend to leave. High switching costs keep customers even when a competitor is somewhat better, because leaving is not free.
- **Marketing use**: lower the switching cost of coming to you (easy migration, onboarding help, import tools) and raise the honest cost of leaving you (accumulated data, integrations, learned workflow that genuinely serves them).
- **Honest use**: earn stickiness by making staying genuinely better, not by holding the customer's data hostage or hiding the exit. Lock-in that survives only because leaving is punished is a liability waiting to churn.
- **Backfire**: retention propped up by switching costs alone masks a product people would leave if they easily could. It reads as healthy until a rival removes the friction, then it collapses at once.

### Exploration vs exploitation
- **Mechanism**: with limited resource you must both exploit what already works and explore for something better. All exploit and you get stuck on today's best; all explore and you never bank the gains. The right split shifts as you learn.
- **Marketing use**: run proven channels and messages to hit the number (exploit) while always testing new ones (explore). Explore more when growth stalls or the environment changes; exploit more when you have found a clear winner and need to cash it in.
- **Honest use**: keep experiments honest and consented (real tests, not manipulative trials on unwitting people), and be straight about which spend is bankable and which is a probe.
- **Backfire**: perpetual exploration is just avoiding commitment with a strategic label, and it never compounds. Perpetual exploitation feels safe right until the winning channel decays and you have no next thing ready.

### Critical mass / tipping point
- **Mechanism**: some effects do nothing until they cross a threshold, then change fast. Below the line, effort seems wasted; above it, the system carries itself.
- **Marketing use**: for anything with a threshold (a network, a community, a brand becoming known in a niche), concentrate resource to punch past the line in one place rather than spreading it thin and stalling below the line everywhere.
- **Honest use**: reach the threshold with real adoption, not inflated vanity numbers. A tipping point faked with bought signals tips back the moment the spend stops.
- **Backfire**: assuming a threshold exists when growth is actually linear makes you dump budget waiting for a takeoff that never comes. And past the tip, momentum can outrun your ability to serve it, so growth turns into churn.

### Survivorship bias
- **Mechanism**: you see the winners and rarely the losers, because failures drop out of view. Studying only survivors makes their traits look like causes of success when they may be noise, or even shared by the failures too.
- **Marketing use**: when copying what worked for a visible success, remember every failed brand that did the same thing and is not around to be studied. Test the tactic on your own case rather than assuming the pattern caused the win.
- **Honest use**: present case studies honestly, including the base rate and the failures, rather than parading survivors as proof of a method. A testimonial reel is not evidence a thing works.
- **Backfire**: it quietly corrupts every "best practice" drawn from famous winners. Build a strategy from survivors only and you are optimising for traits that may have had nothing to do with the outcome.
