# Design and delivery models

The other references cover why people feel and choose the way they do. This one covers what you do about it: how to shape a funnel, a flow, or a single moment so the action you want actually happens. These are behaviour-design and delivery frameworks, not persuasion tricks. Persuasion changes the mind; delivery changes the path. Most conversions die on the path, not the mind, which is why this is the reference you reach for most.

Every model below is well known and generic. Each one is also over-applied in the wild, so each entry ends with the failure mode. Use them to remove friction from an action that is genuinely good for the person taking it. The moment you use them to smooth the path to something bad for that person, they stop being design and become dark patterns; that boundary lives in the ethics reference.

## Fewer options, faster decisions (the more-choices-slower-decisions law)

**Mechanism.** Decision time rises with the number and complexity of the options on offer. Every extra choice adds comparison work, and past a small handful the person stalls, defers, or leaves rather than commit.

**Marketing use.** Cut the options at each decision point. Three pricing tiers, not nine. One primary call to action per screen, not five competing buttons. Curated shortlists and "recommended" defaults do the sorting the visitor was dreading. Fewer, clearer paths almost always convert better than a complete menu.

**Backfire / watch-out.** Fewer is not always better. Strip too far and you hide the option someone actually needed, or you look thin next to a competitor. The real lever is comparison difficulty, not raw count: ten near-identical items filtered well can feel easier than three that force a hard trade-off. Reduce effort, not just quantity.

## The attention-to-action funnel (Attention, Interest, Desire, Action)

**Mechanism.** A classic staged model of how a stranger becomes a customer. Attention: they notice you exist. Interest: they lean in enough to learn more. Desire: they want the specific outcome you sell. Action: they do the thing, buy, sign up, book.

**Marketing use.** A diagnostic ladder. Map where people actually fall off. A traffic problem is an Attention gap. High traffic and low engagement is an Interest gap. Engaged readers who never buy sit stuck between Desire and Action, usually on price, trust, or friction. Fix the stage that is leaking, not the one that is easiest to tweak.

**Backfire / watch-out.** Real journeys are not a tidy one-way staircase. People loop, skip, arrive mid-funnel from a review, and buy on a fourth visit. Treat it as a checklist of things that must be true, not a fixed sequence every person marches through. Obsessing over the top of the funnel while the bottom leaks is the most common way to waste a budget.

## Repeated exposure before action (the several-touchpoints rule)

**Mechanism.** People rarely act on a first encounter. It takes multiple exposures across time and channels before a message registers, earns trust, and gets acted on. The exact number is folklore, but the direction is real: repetition builds familiarity, and familiarity lowers resistance.

**Marketing use.** Plan for the campaign, not the impression. Retargeting, email sequences, and consistent presence across the places your audience already is all exist because one touch is almost never enough. Judge a channel on its contribution to an eventual conversion, not on a single click.

**Backfire / watch-out.** Repetition without spacing or variety becomes irritation, and irritation breeds active avoidance: the mute, the unsubscribe, the ad-blocker. There is also no magic count; treating a specific number as a target leads to spraying more of the same message instead of improving it. Frequency helps a good message and accelerates the death of a bad one.

## Nudges and choice architecture

**Mechanism.** How options are arranged changes what people pick, even when the options themselves do not change. Defaults, ordering, framing, and what is made salient all steer behaviour while leaving every choice available. People overwhelmingly stick with whatever is pre-selected.

**Marketing use.** Design the environment, not just the offer. Set the sensible plan as the default. Put the option most people want where the eye lands first. Frame a price as a small daily figure. Pre-tick the choice that serves the customer, such as the safer shipping or the plan that fits their stated need.

**Backfire / watch-out.** This is the single most abused idea in the list. A default that serves the customer is good architecture; a default that quietly adds cost, hides an opt-out, or pre-selects the thing they would refuse if asked plainly is a dark pattern. The test is simple and non-negotiable: would the person thank you once they see clearly what the arrangement did? If not, do not ship it.

## B = MAP (Behaviour happens when Motivation, Ability, and a Prompt meet)

**Mechanism.** A behaviour occurs only when three things line up at the same moment: enough motivation to act, enough ability to act, and a prompt that triggers the action right then. Remove any one and nothing happens. The practical insight is that when motivation is scarce, raising ability, making the action easier, is usually faster and cheaper than trying to inflate desire.

**Marketing use.** When an action is not happening, diagnose which of the three is missing. No prompt: add the reminder, the button, the timely email. Low ability: cut steps, remove fields, pre-fill, simplify. Low motivation: sharpen the value or, better, make the ask so small that the little motivation on hand is already enough. Prompt people when both their motivation and their ability are highest, not on your schedule.

**Backfire / watch-out.** Prompting someone who has neither the motivation nor the ability to act is just spam, and it burns permission you will want later. And the model is amoral: it makes any behaviour easier to trigger, including ones the person will regret. Making an action trivially easy is only a virtue when the action is worth doing.

## EAST (Easy, Attractive, Social, Timely)

**Mechanism.** A delivery checklist. Behaviour is more likely when the action is Easy (low effort, few steps, a strong default), Attractive (it draws the eye and the offer is clearly worth it), Social (people can see that others like them do it), and Timely (it lands at the moment the person is able and willing to act).

**Marketing use.** Run any ask through the four before you ship it. Easy: how many clicks and fields can you delete? Attractive: does the value read in the first glance? Social: can you show real usage or genuine numbers? Timely: is this arriving at a useful moment or an arbitrary one? It is a fast, memorable audit for onboarding, emails, and checkout.

**Backfire / watch-out.** The letters compete. Maximum Attractive can wreck Easy with clutter. Forced Social with invented or trivial counts reads as manipulation and kills trust. Timely curdles into intrusive when your idea of the right moment is really just your quarter-end. Balance the four; do not max one and call it optimised.

## COM-B (Capability, Opportunity, Motivation drive Behaviour)

**Mechanism.** For a behaviour to happen, three conditions must all hold: Capability (the person can physically and mentally do it), Opportunity (the environment and timing allow it), and Motivation (they want to, including habit and impulse, not just reasoned intent). A gap in any one blocks the behaviour, regardless of how strong the other two are.

**Marketing use.** A broader diagnostic than a funnel. Someone abandoning a signup may want the product (Motivation fine) but be defeated by a confusing form (Capability gap) or hit a paywall on mobile at the wrong moment (Opportunity gap). It stops you from throwing motivation, more ads, louder copy, at what is actually an ability or context problem.

**Backfire / watch-out.** The usual failure is reflexively attacking Motivation because persuasion feels like the marketer's job, while the real blocker is a Capability or Opportunity gap that copy cannot fix. Diagnose honestly before you spend. A perfectly motivated customer still cannot buy through a broken checkout.

## Activation energy (lower the cost of the first step)

**Mechanism.** Borrowed from chemistry: a reaction needs an initial push to get going, and behaviour is the same. The first step carries most of the perceived cost. Once someone starts, momentum and the pull toward finishing what they began carry them further than the starting effort would predict.

**Marketing use.** Make the first step almost weightless. "Start free, no card" beats "Buy now". A progress bar already one step filled. A pre-populated draft instead of a blank page. Ask for the smallest possible commitment first and let the sequence deepen it. Shrinking the entry cost usually lifts completion more than any downstream tweak.

**Backfire / watch-out.** A frictionless first step that leads into a heavy or dishonest second step just moves the drop-off and adds resentment, the classic "free trial that demands a card and buries the cancel". Low-commitment starts also pull in low-intent users, so a jump in signups can hide flat real value. Lower the true cost of proceeding, not just the visible cost of starting.

## The one-metric-that-matters (the guiding metric)

**Mechanism.** A single metric chosen to capture the real value your product delivers to customers, used to align a team and cut through vanity numbers. Done right it rises only when customers genuinely get what they came for, so improving it and serving people point the same way.

**Marketing use.** Pick the metric that reflects delivered value, not surface activity: weekly active users who complete the core action, not raw signups; repeat purchases, not one-off traffic spikes. Then let it settle arguments. Everyone can see whether a change moved the thing that actually matters, which kills a lot of politics.

**Backfire / watch-out.** Any single metric becomes a target, and a target gets gamed. Choose a shallow proxy, page views, registrations, and teams will optimise the proxy while real value stalls or drops. The metric must sit close to genuine customer value, and even then keep a small set of guardrails so no one wins the number by wrecking retention, trust, or margin.

## The perverse-incentive effect (rewards can produce their opposite)

**Mechanism.** Named for the old story of a bounty paid per dead cobra, which led people to breed cobras for the bounty and made the problem worse. People optimise for the reward you actually pay, not the outcome you meant, and a badly chosen incentive can drive behaviour directly opposite to your goal.

**Marketing use.** A warning to hold over every incentive, commission, KPI, referral bounty, and growth target before you launch it. Reward closed deals and support may start closing bad-fit customers who churn. Reward tickets resolved and quality quietly drops. Ask what the cheapest way to hit this number is, then assume someone will find it.

**Backfire / watch-out.** The whole model is the backfire, so the discipline is to test incentives small before you scale them and to watch the second-order effects, not just the headline number. Pair every incentive with a guardrail that catches the gamed version. An incentive that looks brilliant on a slide is exactly the kind that gets exploited in the field.

## The line these models sit on

Every model here does one thing: it makes a chosen action easier to take. That is neutral machinery. Point it at an action that is genuinely good for the person, a clearer choice, a lighter first step, a reminder they wanted, and it is honest design. Point the identical tools at an action that is bad for them, a default that costs them money, a first step that traps them, a metric that rewards churning them, and the same machinery becomes a dark pattern. Nothing in the mechanism tells you which way you are pointing it; that judgement lives in the ethics reference, and it is not optional.
