# Persuasion and Pricing

A working reference for the levers that move people. Every one of them is neutral. It becomes fair or manipulative based on whether the claim underneath it is true. The pattern to hold throughout: the technique is never the problem, the lie you dress up with it is.

## Persuasion and influence

### Reciprocity

- **Mechanism.** People feel an obligation to return a favour. Give something first and the receiver carries a small debt until they even it out.
- **Marketing use.** Free samples, a genuinely useful guide, a tool that solves a problem before any ask, a discount that feels like a gift rather than a coupon.
- **Honest use.** The gift has to be real value the person keeps whether or not they buy. No strings hidden in the fine print, no "free" that bills you in thirty days.
- **Backfire.** A cheap trinket engineered only to trigger guilt reads as a bribe, and people resent being managed. Oversized favours that demand an oversized return feel like a trap and kill trust.

### Commitment and consistency (small yes before big yes)

- **Mechanism.** Once someone takes a small public step, they want later actions to line up with it. A tiny first agreement makes the next one feel natural.
- **Marketing use.** A free trial before a plan, a short quiz before a signup, a newsletter before a purchase. Each yes lowers the friction on the next.
- **Honest use.** Each step has to stand on its own merit, and the person can stop at any point without penalty or shame. The small yes must not quietly pre-commit them to something they never saw coming.
- **Backfire.** Engineering a trivial agreement only to trap someone into a larger one they would have refused up front is a con. Subscriptions that are easy to start and deliberately hard to leave weaponise this and breed cancellations plus chargebacks.

### Authority

- **Mechanism.** People defer to credible expertise and to visible signals of it. A qualified voice shortcuts the reader's own evaluation.
- **Marketing use.** Real credentials, named specialists, published test results, certifications, a track record shown plainly.
- **Honest use.** The authority must be genuine and relevant to the claim. A real expert on the actual subject, not a borrowed lab coat or a stock photo with a title under it.
- **Backfire.** Fake or borrowed authority is fraud, and it collapses the moment one buyer checks. Even real authority used to intimidate rather than inform ("experts agree, so stop asking") pushes thoughtful people away.

### Liking and similarity

- **Mechanism.** People say yes more readily to those they like and to those who seem like them. Warmth and common ground lower defences.
- **Marketing use.** A brand voice that sounds like the reader, founders who share the customer's background, communities built around a shared interest.
- **Honest use.** The similarity is real, not a costume. You actually understand and share something with this audience, and the liking rests on that rather than on flattery.
- **Backfire.** Manufactured sameness, mirroring someone only to sell to them, reads as insincere the instant it slips. Hollow flattery makes the eventual pitch feel like the whole point of the friendliness.

### Unity and shared identity

- **Mechanism.** Stronger than mere liking. People act for a group they count themselves part of, treating its interests as their own.
- **Marketing use.** Membership language, a named community, "people like us" framing, products that signal belonging.
- **Honest use.** The shared identity has to be one the person genuinely chose and the group has to actually serve them. Belonging is offered, never faked, and the group's interest and the member's interest really do line up.
- **Backfire.** Weaponised identity turns into tribalism and us-versus-them selling, where questioning the product feels like betraying the group. That silences honest doubt and eventually curdles into distrust of the whole thing.

### Scarcity and urgency

- **Mechanism.** People value what is rare or running out, and a deadline forces a decision before deliberation talks them out of it.
- **Marketing use.** Limited runs, genuine low stock, a real closing date on an offer, seasonal availability.
- **Honest use.** The scarcity must be real. If it says three left, there are three left. If the deadline passes, the price actually changes. A limit that resets every time the page reloads is a lie with a clock on it.
- **Backfire.** Fake countdown timers, permanent "ending soon" banners and phantom stock counts are the fastest way to teach an audience that nothing you say is true. Once caught, every future deadline is ignored.

### Foot-in-the-door

- **Mechanism.** A small request accepted makes a larger later request more likely, because the person now sees themselves as the kind of person who says yes to you.
- **Marketing use.** Ask for a small first commitment (a follow, a low-stakes purchase, a short survey) before the larger ask.
- **Honest use.** The small ask must be worth doing on its own, and the later ask has to be presented openly, not smuggled in as if it were the same size. The person always knows what the next step actually costs.
- **Backfire.** When the small yes is bait and the real ask was always ten times bigger, people feel played and pull back further than if you had asked straight. Escalating asks with no exit turn into pressure.

### Door-in-the-face

- **Mechanism.** A large request refused makes a smaller follow-up feel reasonable by contrast, and the retreat looks like a concession the person should match.
- **Marketing use.** Present a premium option first so the standard one lands as sensible and affordable.
- **Honest use.** The large option has to be a real thing someone could genuinely want and buy, not a fake high price invented only to make the next number look kind. Both options must be honestly priced on their own.
- **Backfire.** A deliberately absurd opening ask exposes the trick and makes the "concession" feel manufactured. People sense the manipulation and distrust the smaller number too.

### Loss aversion

- **Mechanism.** Losing something hurts more than gaining the same thing pleases. People work harder to avoid a loss than to secure an equal gain.
- **Marketing use.** Free trials that become theirs to keep or lose, framing around what the person misses by not acting, protecting gains already made.
- **Honest use.** The loss described has to be a genuine cost the person would actually bear. Point to real downsides, not invented ones, and never manufacture a fear that does not exist to sell relief from it.
- **Backfire.** Fear-mongering, inventing threats so you can sell the cure, is manipulation at its plainest. It exhausts people, and the ones who see through it hold the fabricated fear against you.

### Anchoring

- **Mechanism.** The first number seen sets the reference point every later number is judged against. High anchors make what follows feel cheap.
- **Marketing use.** Show the original price beside the sale price, lead with the top tier, quote a comparison cost the reader already accepts.
- **Honest use.** The anchor must be genuine. A "was" price has to be a price the item actually sold at, not a fictional high struck through to fake a discount. A real reference, not a decorative one.
- **Backfire.** Invented list prices and permanent "50% off" of a number nobody ever paid are a well-known deception, and in many places illegal. Once buyers learn the anchor is fake, the sale price loses all pull.

### The decoy effect

- **Mechanism.** Adding a third, deliberately worse option changes which of the other two people choose, by making one of them look clearly superior.
- **Marketing use.** A third tier positioned so the target option becomes the obvious best value in the set.
- **Honest use.** Every option, including the decoy, must be a real, honest offer a person could reasonably choose and be well served by. The decoy is a genuine product, not a broken one placed to trick the eye.
- **Backfire.** A decoy engineered to be pure junk, existing only to herd people, insults the buyer who notices, and anyone who picks it gets a bad deal you designed on purpose. That is a complaint and a refund waiting to happen.

### Framing

- **Mechanism.** The same fact feels different depending on how it is worded. Ninety percent lean and ten percent fat describe one thing and land in opposite ways.
- **Marketing use.** Lead with the true angle that matters most to the reader, choose the accurate wording that makes a real benefit legible.
- **Honest use.** Every frame has to be factually true and not hide the part that matters. Framing chooses which true thing to say first, it never says a false thing or buries a material downside the person needs to weigh.
- **Backfire.** Framing that technically avoids lying while steering people away from a fact they would care about is deception by omission. When the hidden side surfaces, it reads as a betrayal, not a clever angle.

### Contrast (show the before state)

- **Mechanism.** A change reads more strongly when the starting point is visible. The gap between before and after does the persuading.
- **Marketing use.** Before-and-after states, the problem shown clearly before the solution, the old slow way next to the new one.
- **Honest use.** The before must be an honest depiction of the real starting point, and the after a result the product actually delivers for a normal user. No staged misery, no cherry-picked best-ever outcome sold as typical.
- **Backfire.** Exaggerated or faked before states, and after results no real customer reaches, set an expectation the product cannot meet. The buyer's own experience becomes the rebuttal.

### Positive framing

- **Mechanism.** Emphasising what someone gains, in affirmative language, feels better and builds more willingness than a negative or fearful framing of the same fact.
- **Marketing use.** Describe the outcome the person wants, the capability they gain, the good result, in confident and specific terms.
- **Honest use.** The positive must be real and achievable, not inflated. Optimism about a true benefit is fair. Optimism about a benefit that will not materialise is just a nicer lie.
- **Backfire.** Relentless positivity used to paper over real limitations or risks the buyer needs to know sets up disappointment. Toothless hype also stops being believed when every product sounds the same.

## Pricing psychology

### Charm pricing / left-digit effect

- **Mechanism.** People read prices left to right and weight the first digit heavily, so 9.99 feels meaningfully cheaper than 10.00 even though the gap is a cent. The dropped leading digit does the work.
- **Marketing use.** Ending value-focused prices in .99 or .95 to sit just under a round threshold.
- **Honest use.** The price is what it is, and the total the person pays is shown clearly with no surprise fees stacked after the charming number. The cent below a round figure is a fair nudge as long as nothing is hidden behind it.
- **Backfire.** Overused, .99 everything signals bargain-basement and can cheapen a premium brand. Worse, a low sticker followed by fees at checkout is a bait that produces abandoned carts and angry buyers.

### Rounded-price fluency (round reads premium, .99 reads value)

- **Mechanism.** A clean round price is easy to process and feels confident and considered, which the mind associates with quality. A .99 price feels calculated and signals a deal. The format itself carries a message before the number does.
- **Marketing use.** Round prices for premium and emotional purchases, .99 endings for value and rational purchases. Match the format to what the product is actually about.
- **Honest use.** The format signals a positioning the product genuinely holds. A round premium price on something that is actually premium is fair, and the same round number on a poor product is a hollow costume the experience will contradict.
- **Backfire.** A premium round price on a mediocre product raises expectations the product cannot meet, which reads as arrogance and drives refunds. Format alone cannot carry a value that is not there.

### The rule of 100

- **Mechanism.** The bigger discount number feels larger, whether or not it truly is. Under 100, a percentage looks bigger than the money. Over 100, the money looks bigger than the percentage. 20% off 50 feels stronger than 10 off, while 100 off 500 feels stronger than 20%.
- **Marketing use.** Show percentages on lower-priced items and absolute amounts on higher-priced ones, so the same real discount reads as generously as it honestly can.
- **Honest use.** The discount is real and both ways of stating it are true and equal. You are choosing the more vivid honest expression of a genuine saving, not inflating the saving itself.
- **Backfire.** The choice of format tips into deception only when the underlying discount is fake, for example a percentage off an inflated anchor. Then the vivid number is dressing on a lie and does the same damage as any phantom discount.

### Good-better-best / price relativity

- **Mechanism.** Given three tiers, most people avoid the cheapest for fear of missing out and the dearest for fear of overpaying, and settle in the middle. The tiers exist partly to make the middle feel like the sensible choice.
- **Marketing use.** Three options built so the target plan sits in the middle, with a clear reason to prefer it over the entry tier and a top tier that frames it as good value.
- **Honest use.** All three tiers are real, fairly priced, and each is the right choice for some genuine customer. The entry tier is not deliberately crippled to force the upgrade, and the top tier is a real product, not a prop.
- **Backfire.** A gutted cheap tier designed only to be rejected, or a middle tier that is not actually the best value it pretends to be, punishes whoever trusts the layout. People who feel funnelled into the "chosen" option resent the design once they spot it.

### Mental-accounting reframes (per-day vs per-year)

- **Mechanism.** The same cost feels different sliced differently. A euro a day sounds trivial where 365 a year sounds like a decision. Breaking a price into small familiar units lowers the felt pain of paying.
- **Marketing use.** Express a subscription or large purchase in its smallest fair unit ("less than a coffee a day") to make the true cost easier to grasp and accept.
- **Honest use.** The full price is still shown plainly and the small unit maths is accurate. The per-day figure helps someone understand a real cost, it does not hide the annual total or the fact that they are committing to the whole thing.
- **Backfire.** Quoting only the per-day slice while burying the yearly commitment, or the auto-renewal, is how people end up locked into costs they never added up. The reckoning comes on the bank statement, and with it the cancellation.

## A closing note

These are the levers where persuasion most easily tips into manipulation, because most of them work just as well on a false claim as on a true one. A fake countdown persuades. A phantom anchor persuades. A gutted cheap tier persuades. That is exactly why the rule is the same for every entry above: use the lever only when the thing underneath it is true. Real scarcity, genuine anchors, honest tiers, accurate maths. For where the line sits and how to hold it under pressure, see the ethics reference.
